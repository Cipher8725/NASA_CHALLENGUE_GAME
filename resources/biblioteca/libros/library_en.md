# NaSA-GAME Astronaut Study Library

## The Solar System
*Beginner*

### Our Cosmic Address

Earth is a planet in the solar system. The Sun is the star at its center, and the entire solar system is part of the Milky Way galaxy. A moon is a natural satellite that orbits a planet or another body.

### The Planets

Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are rocky planets. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants. Uranus and Neptune are ice giants. Pluto is a dwarf planet.

### Small Bodies

Many asteroids are made of rock or metal. Comets contain ice and dust. As a comet approaches the Sun, some of its ice turns into gas, which can help form a tail.

## Earth and the Moon
*Beginner*

### Rotation and Orbit

Earth spins on its axis, creating the usual cycle of day and night. The Moon takes about as long to spin once as it takes to orbit Earth. That is why we nearly always see the same side of the Moon.

### Phases and Eclipses

The Moon's phases depend on how much of its sunlit half we can see from Earth. A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun. A lunar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes through Earth's shadow.

### The Lunar Environment

Gravity at the Moon's surface is about one-sixth as strong as Earth's. Your mass stays the same, but you weigh less. The Moon has an extremely thin exosphere, and its surface is covered with dust and rock fragments called regolith.

## Living in Space
*Beginner*

### Why Astronauts Float

In orbit, the spacecraft and everyone inside it fall together around Earth. This shared free fall makes astronauts feel weightless. The term microgravity reflects the small accelerations and disturbances that still occur.

### Staying Fit

With less physical loading, muscles and bones can weaken. Astronauts do resistance and aerobic exercise to reduce these effects.

### Working as a Crew

The crew's schedule sets aside time for maintenance, experiments, meals, exercise, and rest. Astronauts secure tools and equipment so they do not float away and follow written procedures to coordinate their work.

## From Liftoff to Orbit
*Intermediate*

### Thrust and Weight

A rocket expels mass at high speed and produces thrust in the opposite direction. To lift off vertically, its thrust must exceed its weight. Gravity and air resistance affect its path during flight.

### Rocket Stages

Each stage carries its own propulsion system and propellant. Some rockets drop a spent stage so the remaining stages can accelerate with less mass. The payload is what the rocket is intended to carry.

### Reaching Orbit

A vehicle must gain altitude and build up enough sideways speed. Gravity bends its path. At the right speed for its altitude, the spacecraft keeps falling around Earth.

## Anatomy of a Rover
*Intermediate*

### Crossing Rough Terrain

The chassis supports the equipment, and the wheels transfer driving force to the ground. Perseverance uses an articulated suspension to help its wheels stay in contact with uneven terrain.

### Seeing and Measuring

Navigation cameras show the terrain, while scientific instruments study materials and the surrounding environment. Perseverance uses a robotic arm to position instruments near rocks and help collect samples.

### Power and Communications

A rover needs electricity to move, process data, and communicate. Perseverance uses a thermoelectric generator and batteries. It can send data directly to Earth or relay it through orbiters, depending on the communication link.

## Life Support
*Intermediate*

### A Breathable Cabin

A cabin needs suitable pressure and a breathable mix of gases. Sensors monitor cabin conditions, ventilation circulates the air, and purification equipment removes carbon dioxide and other contaminants.

### Recycling Water and Making Oxygen

On the International Space Station, water is recovered from cabin humidity and wastewater, purified, and checked for quality. Electrolysis can split water into oxygen and hydrogen. The oxygen can then be supplied to the cabin.

### How Systems Depend on Each Other

Pumps, fans, heaters, and sensors need electricity. Cabin systems also regulate temperature. A power loss can affect several pieces of equipment at once, which is why reserves and backup systems matter.

## Your Body in Space
*Intermediate*

### Eating Well Far from Earth

Your body needs energy and nutrients in space too. Astronaut menus have between 1,900 and 3,200 calories a day. The Space Station receives fresh food every few months, but on the Moon or Mars it will have to be grown.

### Lettuce on the Station

Red romaine lettuce grew in the Veggie garden on the ISS. It was harvested after 33 days. Scientists found no microbes dangerous to people and saw that it had as many antioxidants as lettuce grown on Earth. The astronauts ate part of the harvest (study OSD-745).

### Red Light or Blue Light

In 2019, mizuna was grown under two kinds of light: 90% red and 10% blue, or half and half. The harvest and its chemistry changed with the light. In the first attempt the fertilizer was too strong and damaged the plants; in the second it was reduced and the plants did better (studies OSD-780 and OSD-655).

## Lunar Exploration
*Advanced*

### After Landing

The crew checks the spacecraft and its communications, reviews the available time and supplies, and assesses the terrain before going outside. Checklists depend on the vehicle and mission.

### Planning the Route

Slopes, deep shadows, and loose regolith affect mobility and visibility. A route should include landmarks, a return plan, and reserves of time and consumables.

### Documenting a Sample

A rock's location, photographs, and observations of its surroundings provide scientific context. A unique ID connects the sample to that record. Proper storage helps protect it from contamination and prevents mix-ups.

## Mission Control
*Advanced*

### Reading Telemetry

Telemetry transmits measurements such as pressure, temperature, and voltage. To interpret a reading, check its units, its timestamp, its trend, and its expected range for that phase of the mission.

### Connecting Alerts

Several alerts can share a cause. If a voltage drop coincides with faults in equipment powered by the same supply, compare when the events occurred with other readings and check the status of the backup systems.

### Reporting What You Observe

A concise report identifies the equipment, when the event occurred, what values were observed, and what has been confirmed. The crew and specialists use shared data and procedures to coordinate decisions.

## Spacesuits
*Advanced*

### A Portable Habitat

A spacesuit for extravehicular activity maintains pressure around the body and supplies oxygen. Its life support system also circulates air and removes the carbon dioxide the astronaut exhales.

### Cooling, Protection, and Mobility

Physical activity produces heat. A garment with circulating water can carry that heat away. The outer layers and visor protect the astronaut, while the joints help the astronaut move in a pressurized suit.

### Saving Enough for the Return

Battery power, oxygen, and other consumables limit how long an astronaut can stay outside. Communications and suit monitoring help the crew adjust the route and preserve a reserve for the return trip.

## Space Weather
*Advanced*

### Solar Flares

The Sun releases bursts of energy called flares. They are classified with letters: C, M and X, and each letter is 10 times stronger than the one before. On February 1, 2026, active region 14366 on the Sun produced an X8.1 flare, recorded by NASA's DONKI database.

### Radiation Measured on the ISS

The DosTel detectors on the Space Station measured radiation on April 1, 2022. In 24 hours, an astronaut received between 250 and 290 microgray. Most of the time the rate was close to 4 microgray per hour, but several times a day it rose to hundreds (RadLab, NASA).

### Watching the Sun

When a strong flare happens, DONKI sends an alert with the time, the class and the region of the Sun, and warns whether more energetic protons may arrive. In the Moon Base, those same real flares damage the shield and you decide when to repair it.
